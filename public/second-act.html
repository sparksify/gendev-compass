Why One Retired Executive Started Looking at “Boring” B2B Businesses Instead of Another Corporate Job

After leaving the C-suite, Mike Reynolds wasn’t looking for another title. He was looking for something worth building. What he discovered led him into a business category most executives rarely think about.

By The Second Act Report Partner Story September 2026 · 7 min read

For most executives, retirement is supposed to be the finish line.

Mike Reynolds discovered something different.

He didn’t miss the meetings.

He didn’t miss crowded airports, quarterly forecasts or calendars measured in fifteen-minute increments.

What he missed was having something to build.

After spending years making decisions, leading people and solving problems, doing absolutely nothing sounded considerably better in theory than it felt in practice.

But returning to corporate America wasn’t the answer either.

And that created an interesting question:

What kind of business actually makes sense for somebody who has spent an entire career learning how to lead—but has no desire to start another career?

The Question That Changed His Search

At first, Reynolds looked where most prospective business owners look.

Consumer businesses.

Popular franchises.

Restaurants.

Retail concepts.

The kinds of businesses everyone recognizes immediately.

Then he began asking a different question:

“Instead of asking what consumers WANT to buy, what do businesses repeatedly NEED handled?”

That changed the search.

Some of the least glamorous categories suddenly became some of the most interesting.

Payroll.

Maintenance.

Safety.

Compliance.

Testing.

Services that exist behind the scenes of American business.

They’re rarely dinner-table conversation.

But every day, employers hire people, manage workplace-safety procedures, respond to incidents and administer testing programs.

And those responsibilities don’t disappear because a category isn’t fashionable.

The Attraction of “Boring”

For Reynolds, this created a different way to evaluate business ownership.

He wasn’t searching for the trendiest concept.

He wanted to understand businesses built around several characteristics:

A business-to-business customer base.

Services tied to real operating needs.

The ability to build ongoing employer relationships.

A model where leadership, business development and execution mattered.

And a system he wouldn’t have to invent completely from scratch.

That search eventually brought him to the workplace-testing industry.

A Business Most People Never Think About

What Mike Found Interesting

  • Business-to-business customers
  • Mobile service model
  • Employer relationships
  • Workplace testing and compliance needs
  • Systemized franchise structure

Source: CMDT franchise materials and interviews.

Complete Mobile Drug Testing—CMDT—provides mobile drug and alcohol testing and related workplace services to businesses and organizations.

Rather than requiring customers to build their day around visiting a traditional testing location, the mobile model brings services to employers and their workforce.

The company has developed its franchise model around workplace testing services and the continuing needs of business customers.

CMDT’s own franchise materials describe the model as designed around growing demand for fast, reliable and compliant workplace testing solutions, with a structure intended to help franchise partners operate efficiently and build recurring, scalable revenue.

But there’s an important distinction.

This is not being presented as passive income.

Owning a business still means building relationships, developing a territory and executing.

For the right former executive, that may actually be part of its appeal.

The very skills corporate life spent years developing—leadership, accountability, relationship-building, business development and execution—don’t suddenly become useless after the final board meeting.

“They can become the operating system for something you own.”
